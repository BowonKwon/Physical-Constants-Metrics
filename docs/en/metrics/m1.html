M1

Relative-uncertainty half-life

Years for the relative uncertainty to halve

Takeaway
  • The relative standard uncertainty (the error-bar width relative to the value) halved roughly every 5.4 years: the median half-life over the 16 core constants whose uncertainty declined.
  • The electron relative atomic mass has the shortest half-life (3.0 years) and the Newtonian constant of gravitation the longest (12.2 years).
Definition
For each constant, how fast the relative standard uncertainty (u/|x| — the error-bar width relative to the value) fell. log₂ of the relative standard uncertainty is regressed on edition year, giving the years for the uncertainty to halve (the half-life).
Formula
log₂(u/|x|) = a + b·year, half-life = −1/b (years). The 95% interval maps b ± t(0.975, n−2)·SE(b) through the same expression (a regression interval, not a bootstrap). A slope flatter than −0.001 per year (half-life above 1,000 years) is reported as no decline.
Denominator
Constants with a measured (not exact) value in at least four editions: 17 core constants, 28 including display constants.
How to read it
Over the 16 core constants whose uncertainty declined, the median half-life is 5.4 years: roughly every 5.4 years the relative standard uncertainty halved (5.7 years over all 17, counting the one with no decline). The electron relative atomic mass has the shortest half-life (3.0 years) and the Newtonian constant of gravitation the longest (12.2 years). The neutron magnetic moment kept almost the same uncertainty from 1986 on (slope −7×10⁻¹¹ per year, interval including zero) and is shown as “no decline.”
Caveats
Editions are 4 to 13 years apart. Constants that became exact along the way (fixed by definition rather than measured), such as the Planck and Boltzmann constants, are counted only up to that point. A straight line is a summary: constants whose uncertainty dropped in one step when a new method arrived fit it poorly (low R²).
